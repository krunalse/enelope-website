## Where AI actually helps in manufacturing

Swiss manufacturers are cautious about AI, and with reason. An ETH Zurich survey with Swissmem and Next Industries, covering over 200 tech-industry companies, found that adoption is hardly implemented beyond initial pilot tests. For production use, half of respondents had not considered AI, and only 10 percent were running pilots. Only 25 percent reported having an AI strategy.

The same study names the obstacles: two-thirds of companies said they have no or limited in-house AI skills, and many lack data governance and suitable IT infrastructure. The main driver is practical, efficiency in specific business problems rather than following trends. Smaller and less profitable firms were the ones falling behind.

That suggests where to begin. Machine learning on the shop floor needs sensors, clean data and integration. Often the faster win is around the plant: customer and technical support, quoting, spare-part questions, maintenance knowledge locked in manuals and the heads of senior technicians, and compliance paperwork. The study lists customer service, tech support and sales among the areas companies are exploring.

## What we build

### Technical support assistant
A chatbot for customers and field staff that answers from your manuals, datasheets and service bulletins, in the language the user writes in, and cites the page. If it is unsure, it opens a ticket with the details already collected.

### Quote and order intake agent
An agent that reads incoming enquiries and drawings or specification emails, extracts the requirements, checks them against your catalogue, and prepares a draft quote for a sales engineer to approve.

### Maintenance knowledge assistant
A private search over service reports, past faults and procedures, so a technician can ask what was done the last time a machine showed a given fault. It supports the person on site and does not control equipment.

### Quality and compliance document agent
An agent that assembles declarations, test records and traceability documents from your systems and flags gaps before audits, with a quality manager approving the final version.

### Edge-to-cloud data foundation
Where a predictive use case is justified, we help set up the data pipeline and cloud environment first, in Switzerland or the EU, and tell you honestly if your data is not yet ready.

## Compliance and data considerations

Most of the above handles business and technical documents, but they can contain personal data and trade secrets. The Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection (nFADP/revDSG) applies directly to AI-supported processing, according to the FDPIC. For intellectual property, we recommend Swiss or EU hosting, no use of your data to train third-party models, and strict access control on drawings and formulas.

Product regulation matters if AI touches machinery itself. According to EU-OSHA, the EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 applies from 20 January 2027 and is meant to better cover technologies such as autonomous machinery, connected equipment and AI-based modules that ensure safety functions. Swiss exporters selling into the EU should plan for it. We keep our assistants away from safety functions. The EU AI Act implementation timeline also sets out staged obligations, with Annex I high-risk requirements applying from 2 August 2028.

In practice that means audit logs of what the assistant answered and from which source, human approval before a quote or compliance document is released, and a clear separation between advisory tools and anything that controls a machine.

## How an engagement starts

We start with a scoping session to find one process with a measurable cost, such as time to answer a support request or time to prepare a quote, and run a small pilot against that baseline. If your data or processes are not ready, we will say so and suggest the preparation first. Where the pilot works, we extend it to the next process.
